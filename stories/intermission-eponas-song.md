# Intermission: Epona's Song

*No fight this time.* Link comes back to the bar six years older than he left it. He has saved two worlds, and in the one he went home to he stands the night watch on the castle wall. He has a wife, and a son who picked up a stick last week and did something nobody taught him. The room has a good deal to say about that, and for once Link says something back. It comes after [The Smallest Cut](musashi-vs-alfa.md).

---

**Arthur**

The boy looked older.

Arthur noticed it before anybody else did. He was the one who drew faces. Link came through the door in the green, with the sword on his back and the shield over it, the same as always, and walked down to his stool beside the shadow. But there was a ring pressed into his hair at the temples that a helmet makes, worn in over a long time. His hands had the calluses a man gets from a pole, not a sword. The softness had gone out of his jaw. The empty bottle still hung at his belt.

The old man set down the milk. Then, beside it, a second bottle. It was small and corked, the kind a child could get both hands round.

"It's paid for."

Link looked at the small bottle a long time. He did not touch it.

The shadow beside him set its glass of still water down at the same moment Link set down his milk. It did not look at the small bottle. It looked at Link.

Arthur put his pencil away. "You look older'n you did last week, son."

Link drank. "Six years."

Spike turned round on his stool. "Six *what*?"

"Years."

"The door does that," said the priest, from down the bar. He said it gently, the way a man tells you it's raining. "It brings you from wherever you are. You've been somewhere a while, lad."

Arthur turned the stub of a cigarette over on the bar. He had owed this boy a debt for some time. Link had given him a bottle of milk once, the night the cough was bad, and the cough had let go of him for an hour. He had asked where it came from. *Far*, the boy had said, and that had been the end of it.

"That milk," Arthur said. "The night you give it to me. You had a look on you like you was thinking of somebody. There a girl at the end of that, or am I a fool?"

Link was quiet. Arthur did not push him. He had learned long ago that you didn't, with horses or with men like this one. You stood there and you let them come.

"Malon," Link said.

"She the one sings?"

A nod.

"Well, I'll be." Arthur smiled. It didn't come easy to him lately and he let it stay. "You marry her?"

Link turned the milk bottle on the bar. "Yes."

Spike whistled, low. Down the bar the big man with one eye raised his cup an inch and put it down again. That was Guts saying congratulations, and Arthur suspected it was the most he'd ever said it.

"Kids?" Arthur said.

"A boy."

"How old?"

"Five."

It should have been an easy thing to say. Arthur had watched men say it in camps and saloons all his life, with their chests out. Link said it the way a man reads a name off a list.

Arthur waited.

"He picked up a stick," Link said.

He held up his hand, and turned it over, and Arthur saw what the hand was remembering. A small fist round a stick. A boy turning all the way round, once, low, with the stick out flat, and coming back to the place he started.

"Nobody showed him," Link said.

**Link**

He had been nine years old the first time, and the only child in the forest without a fairy. Then he had one, and a sword made for a Kokiri, and a tree that was dying, and a princess in a garden who told him what the tree had not had time to.

He had drawn a sword out of a stone, and the sword had put him to sleep for seven years because he was too small to hold it. He woke up tall in a world that had ended while he slept. He went into six temples and came out of each with someone who had been waiting for him. He went up a tower and came down it while it fell, and fought the thing at the bottom in the rain, with Zelda holding it still. Then she sent him back. She took the seven years away and gave him his own small hands again, and the sword went back into its stone.

He had gone into the garden one more time. He told her all of it, and she believed him. That was the whole of what anybody in Hyrule knew.

Then the fairy left, through a window, and he went looking for her, and he found a land with three days to live and a moon that was coming down on it. He lived the three days so many times that he could have told you what every person in that town was doing at any hour you liked. He wore dead men's faces. He gave the moon back to the sky. Nobody there remembered it either, because he had played the song and taken the days back.

Then he came home.

There was no war. Ganondorf had been taken in chains before he could start one, because a boy had told a princess. So Hyrule had never needed a hero, and Hyrule had no idea it had ever had one. There was a guard post at the castle that took boys from the country who were good with a spear. He was good with a spear. He was good with everything.

He was a knight now, in the end. It meant a helmet and a tabard and a wall. He stood the night watch because nobody else wanted it and because he liked the quiet. From the wall you could see across the field to the ranch. Some nights there was a light in the window, and he knew who had left it there.

He had married her in the spring. Her father cried, and Ingo didn't, and the horse came to the wedding. The first time he went out to the ranch, as a boy, Malon had sung him Epona's Song, and he had played it back to her on the ocarina. Her mother had written it. Now the boy knew it too. He whistled it to the horse, and the horse came, and the boy didn't think anything of that, because to him it had always been so.

The boy had hair like neither of them and both of them, red and gold, like the field at the end of the day.

Last week Link had come down off the watch at dawn and found him at the fence with a stick. He was hitting the posts, the way boys do. Then he had done the other thing. He had turned all the way round once, low, with the stick out flat, and come back to where he started, and every post in reach had felt it. He had laughed out loud.

Link had never done that where the boy could see. He had made sure of it. The real sword was in the Temple of Time, asleep in its stone, and the guard's sword hung on a peg in the barracks, and at home there was nothing on the walls but harness.

He had picked the boy up and carried him in to breakfast. He had not said anything. He had been not saying anything for a week.

"State the probability," said the machine at the end of the bar, "that the offspring will require it."

Link looked down the bar at the red eyes. He thought about how to say it, and that took a while.

"He isn't dead," Link said.

"The offspring?"

"Ganondorf."

The room changed a little at that. It was a room full of people who knew what it meant when somebody said a name like that.

"They tried to execute him," Link said. "It didn't take. He had something they couldn't kill. So they sent him somewhere else instead." He drank the rest of the milk. "I stood at the door. They don't let guards in."

"So he can come back," Arthur said.

Link nodded.

"Then teach him," said the man with the handsome face, from two stools down. He said it brightly, like a man helping. "Come on. You've got a gifted kid, you develop the gift. I had a kid. Gifted. Like, top of the galaxy gifted. And I made sure she used it. Every day. Kept her safe, kept her busy, kept her plugged in. Best thing I ever did for her."

Nobody said anything.

"Where's she now?" Spike said finally.

The mask smiled. It always smiled a moment after the face behind it did. This time there was nothing behind it for it to follow.

"Bandits," Jack said.

He went back to his paper cup. Nobody spoke to him. Kratos had turned his head and was looking at Jack, and went on looking for some time, and Jack did not look up once.

"There's your other road," Guts said. He didn't say it to Jack. He said it to Link. "Somebody taught me. He didn't teach me so I'd be safe. He taught me so I'd be useful. A kid can tell the difference. Took me twenty years to stop tasting it."

Link looked at the small corked bottle. He still had not touched it.

"I don't want him useful," he said.

**Kratos**

The boy in green did not want his son useful.

Kratos understood that better than anyone at this bar. He had said nearly the same words once, with his hand on a door, to nobody. He had meant them more than anything he had ever said.

He had not told his son what he was. He had not told him what his father was, either, or what his father had done, or why his skin was the color of ash. He had taught the boy to hunt, and to track, and to draw a bow, and to close his heart. Those were the useful things. He had not taught him the rest, because the rest was the part that would make him into Kratos, and Kratos had decided that would not happen. He had decided it the way he decided everything, completely, with no room left over for being wrong.

The boy had fallen sick from it.

He had not been wounded. He had been ill, a fever that came and went, worse each time. He had been a god without knowing he was one, and the two halves of him had torn at each other. He nearly died of it in the snow. It was not anything his father taught him that did it. It was everything his father had not.

Kratos had gone to Helheim for the cure. Then he had sat down in front of his son and told him. It was the hardest thing he had ever done, and he had once killed his own father with his hands.

"I kept from my son what he was," Kratos said.

The boy in green looked up. Kratos had not spoken to him before tonight. Very few people at this bar had heard Kratos speak more than a word at a time, and the room had gone quiet to hear it.

"I believed that if he did not know, he could not become it. That I could stand between him and it." Kratos turned his cup a quarter turn on the bar and stopped. "He fell sick with the not knowing. It nearly killed him. Not the knowing."

Link said nothing.

"You will not keep it from him. It is already in him. You saw it at the fence." Kratos looked at the boy's hand, which still lay on the bar the way it had when it showed them the stick going round. "All you choose is whether he learns it from you, or from the day he needs it."

"And if I teach him," Link said, "he goes."

"Perhaps."

"He'll go anyway," said Jin.

He had been sitting on Link's other side all night and had not said a word. That was how it usually went with the two of them.

"I was taught," Jin said. "Everything a samurai learns, I learned. My uncle saw to it. And when I was a boy, my father was killed in front of me, and I had a sword in my hand, and I did nothing with it. I could not move." He did not look at anyone while he said it. "Teaching is not the whole of it. He may be taught everything, and freeze. He may be taught nothing, and stand. You cannot know which until the day comes."

"Then what good is it," Link said.

It was not quite a question. Jin answered it anyway.

"If he is taught, and he freezes," Jin said, "he will live, and someday he will forgive himself, and he will know what to do the next time. I did. If he is not taught, there may not be a next time."

Kratos inclined his head to the samurai. It was more than he gave most people.

Link looked at the small bottle.

**Musashi**

Everyone had said something. The boy in green had answered more of it than Musashi had ever heard him answer, and he looked worn out by it, the way a man does who has been carrying something heavy a long way and has just been asked to set it down where everyone can see.

Musashi had two sons. Neither was his by blood. That was the only way a man like him could come by sons.

The first had been a fine boy, quick with his hands and serious. Musashi had found him a place in a lord's household. He had been glad to go. Then the lord died young, and the boy, who was hardly more than twenty, cut himself open so as to follow his lord into death, because he had been taught that this was what a retainer does. Musashi had not taught him that. He had not taught him otherwise, either. It had not seemed his business. He had been busy with the sword.

The second was twenty-two now and kept a lord's castle in Kokura, and his accounts, and half his council. Musashi had taught him letters and figures and how to sit a horse, and something of the sword, enough. He had decided early that this boy would not live the way his father had lived, and the boy had not. He was the better man of the two of them. Musashi was going to live in his house, when he went back.

He did not know whether he had done right by either one. He had come to think a father never finds that out.

"I had a son," Musashi said, "who was not taught what not to do. Somebody else taught him what to do instead. He died of it, young." He let that sit. "If you do not teach your boy, someone will. A king, or a war, or a man at a bar. You do not choose whether he is taught. Only by whom."

Link was looking at the small bottle.

"I'll teach him the song," he said.

The room waited for the rest of it. There wasn't any.

"The song," Arthur said.

"Epona's Song. Her mother wrote it." Link's hand had gone to the empty bottle at his belt without his seeming to notice. "That's from both of us. That's his."

"And the sword?" said Kratos.

Link didn't answer. That was an answer.

Musashi had known it would go this way since the boy said *I don't want him useful*. He had once decided the same thing about a son of his own and he could not, in honesty, say the boy was wrong. He thought of the son in Kokura, who was safe, and who could keep a castle, and could not have held one.

"Then teach him the song well," Musashi said. "Songs last longer than swords. And leave the rest where he can find it."

Link frowned. "Where?"

"Wherever you are, when he needs it."

Link looked at him as if he had said something in the old court language. Musashi did not explain it. He was not sure himself what he meant. He had found that the true things he said usually came out that way, before he understood them.

He looked at his own hands. He was fifty. He had fought sixty-some times, and had students here and there who had learned a part of it, and had written nothing down. If he died tomorrow, the whole of what he knew would go into the ground with him like water into sand. He had never cared. He had thought a sword was a thing you show a man, not a thing you write.

He had just told a boy to leave it where they could find it.

The old man set an inkstone down in front of him, and a brush, and a clean sheet. Musashi had not asked. He never asked.

---

**Link**

He stood up. The watch would be changing soon. He could feel it the way he could always feel the hour, from Termina, where he had learned every hour there was.

"That's the most I ever heard him say," Spike said to nobody. "In total. That was more than all the rest of it put together."

"Let him be," said the priest.

The shadow had not stood up with him.

That was the first thing Link noticed. It did everything he did, it always had. It drank when he drank. It had sat down when he sat down. Now he was standing, and it was still on its stool, and its hand was resting on the hilt of its sword. His own hand was not on his.

It looked at him with his own face. Its eyes were red. It did not say anything. It never did.

Link looked away first.

Arthur had put a hand out, not to stop him, just to have it there. "Son."

Link waited.

"I had a boy too," Arthur said. "Isaac. Him and his mama. I weren't around much. Sent money when I had it. I was off being useful to some folks." He looked at the cigarette stub and didn't light it. "They was killed for ten dollars while I was gone. I wasn't there to teach him nothing, nor stop it neither."

He took his hand back.

"Whatever you teach that boy or don't," Arthur said, "you be there for it. That's all I know. It ain't much."

Link nodded. He picked up the small corked bottle at last and put it in his pouch, beside the ocarina.

On the way to the door he passed the old swordsman. The brush was wet. At the top of the clean sheet there was one mark, black and still drying, a single character with a great deal of space under it. Link couldn't read it.

"Ground," the swordsman said, without looking up. "One has to start somewhere."

Link opened the door.

It was the wall. It was always the wall, at the end of the watch, with the torch burned down in its bracket and the sky going gray over the field. Nobody had noticed he was gone. Nobody ever had. The helmet was on his head and the pole was in his hand, and the sword on his back was a guard's sword again, plain, from the barracks. The other one was asleep in the temple, where he had left it.

He handed the watch over and walked home across the field.

The ranch was quiet. Malon was already up, out in the barn with the cows, singing. He could hear her from the gate. He went in by the kitchen and up the stairs and stood in the door of the small room.

The boy was asleep on his back with his arms flung out, and the red-gold hair everywhere. The stick was leaning by the bed where he had left it.

Link looked at the stick a long time. Then he left it where it was.

He set the little bottle of milk on the sill. He sat down on the floor by the bed, and took out the ocarina, and played the song very softly, the way you play it to a horse who is already close by. Three notes, and the same three again.

Halfway through the second time the boy began to hum it in his sleep. He had it right. He always had it right.

Out in the field a wolf was howling, a long way off. Link had heard them all his life. He did not think anything of it.
