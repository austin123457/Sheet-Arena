# Intermission: The Regulars

*No fight this time.* Arthur Morgan comes back to the bar after [Dead or Alive](spike-arthur-vs-smasher.md), and finds it isn't empty. It comes after [Come Unto Me](anderson-vs-guts.md).

---

**Arthur**

The sun was all the way up by the time Arthur came back through the door. He didn't remember leaving, exactly. He remembered the satchel, the pen, and the two names. He remembered sitting on the step outside with the sunrise on his face, thinking that if this was where it ended, it was a better place than most.

It hadn't ended. It had just gotten quiet, and then he'd been cold, and then he'd been walking back in.

The bar had company now.

There was a priest at the counter, a big one, in a gray coat with his sleeves rolled up and a little battered book open in front of him. Two stools down from him sat the largest man Arthur had ever seen. He was all scars and black iron, bandaged from his shoulder to his hip, with one eye and a sword leaning against the bar beside him the size of a barn door.

Both of them looked up when he came in.

Arthur stopped in the doorway, his hand resting easy near the Schofield. It was habit. The priest smiled. The big man just looked at him, the way a wolf looks at another wolf.

"Morning," Arthur said.

"Good morning, friend," said the priest. He had a voice like a church organ, and an accent Arthur couldn't place. Scotch, maybe, or something like it. "Come in, come in. You look like a man who's been through the wars."

"One or two." Arthur came in and took the stool on the far side of the big man, leaving the priest a stool of room. The bartender set down a glass of whiskey without a word.

"It's paid for," Arthur said, before the old man could.

The bartender just nodded.

Arthur drank. Then he looked sideways at the big man's bandages. They were clean, careful work, tied off the way a doctor ties them. That surprised him, given the look of the man.

"Somebody patched you up good," he said.

The big man grunted and tilted his head toward the priest.

"Him," he said. "After he finished cutting me apart."

"After *you* finished cutting *me* apart," the priest corrected him cheerfully. "Ten or eleven times. I lost count around the eighth."

Arthur looked from one to the other.

"Y'all are friends, then?"

The big man and the priest looked at each other.

"No," said the big man.

"Not yet," said the priest.

Arthur laughed, and it turned into a cough the way it always did, deep and wet. He bent over the bar and rode it out with his fist against his mouth. When it was done he wiped his hand on his trousers without looking at it.

When he straightened up, the priest was watching him. The smile had gone soft at the edges.

"That's consumption," the priest said quietly.

"Yes, sir."

"How long?"

Arthur shrugged. "Doc said months. That was a while back."

The priest closed his book and turned on his stool to face him.

"I've a gift," he said. "From the Lord. Laying on of hands. It closes wounds, and it's been known to drive out sickness." He held up one big scarred palm. "I'd be glad to try. No charge, and no sermon, I promise you that. Though I'll admit I'm tempted."

Arthur looked at that hand for a long time.

He thought about it, honestly. He thought about John and Abigail and the boy. He thought about Sadie and Charles, and about Mary, and about all the years he might have if he took that hand. Years to do it right this time, maybe.

Then he thought about the Downes family. He thought about Thomas coughing on his porch, and the money Arthur had beaten out of him, and Mrs. Downes's face when she opened the door to him afterward, and the boy.

He shook his head.

"I thank you, Father," he said. "I do. But I reckon I'll keep it."

The priest didn't argue. He looked at Arthur a moment longer, the way you look at a man to see if he's sure.

"It ain't the sickness that needs fixin'," Arthur said. "I got this from a man I did wrong by. Seems fair I carry it."

The priest was quiet. Then he nodded slowly and put his hand on Arthur's shoulder instead, briefly, a big warm weight there and gone.

"Then I'll pray for you," he said. "That one's free too, and you can't stop me."

Arthur smiled despite himself. "No, sir. I don't suppose I can."

The big man had been watching all of it without a word. Now he picked up his cup and drank, and set it down, and said, to nobody in particular:

"He does that."

"Does what?"

"Gets under your skin."

The priest beamed at him.

---

**Arthur**

They'd been sitting there a while, the three of them, when the door opened again.

Arthur didn't turn around. He'd learned not to, in this place. Folks came and went. But he heard the footsteps: light, loose, unhurried, the walk of a man who wasn't sure his legs were going to cooperate and didn't much care either way. He'd heard that walk before, across a stretch of sand, sixty feet away.

He turned around.

Spike Spiegel stood in the doorway in his blue suit, without a mark on him. His hair was a mess and a cigarette was already in his mouth, unlit. He was patting down his pockets for a light.

Arthur stared at him.

Spike found his lighter, lit the cigarette, took a long drag, and looked around the room. He looked at the priest, and the big man with the barn-door sword, and finally at Arthur.

"Hey, cowboy," Spike said. "Did I miss anything?"

Arthur stood up. He didn't remember deciding to. His stool scraped back and almost went over.

"You were dead," he said.

Spike considered this.

"Yeah," he said. "Didn't take."

Arthur crossed the room in three strides. He stood in front of Spike for a long moment, his jaw working and his eyes stinging for no good reason. Then he grabbed the skinny man by the shoulders and held on, hard.

Spike stood there, a little startled, with the cigarette in the corner of his mouth.

"Okay," Spike said. "Okay, I missed you too. You're kind of crushing my ribs."

Arthur let go and stepped back, coughing, and wiped his face with the back of his hand.

"Damn fool," he said. "Going in like that. I told you to get back."

"You did," Spike agreed. "I heard you."

"And you went in anyhow."

"Seemed like the thing to do." Spike looked past him at the bar. "So you got him?"

"I got him."

"Huh." Spike took another drag, looking at Arthur with something that might have been respect. "Nice."

Then something occurred to Arthur, and he scratched the back of his neck.

"Listen. The money."

"Yeah?"

"I, uh. I sent half of it to your friend. Black. Jet Black. On that ship of yours." He looked at the floor. "I put your name on it. Figured that's what you'd want."

Spike was quiet for a moment.

Then he started to laugh. It was a real laugh, the first one Arthur had heard out of him, low and surprised and delighted.

"You sent Jet half of *that*?"

"Seemed fair. You earned it."

"Arthur," Spike said, still laughing, "Jet is going to think I'm dead."

"Well. You was."

"He's going to think I'm dead, and he's going to be standing in the galley holding a bounty bigger than the Bebop's entire life, and he's going to have no idea what to do with it." Spike shook his head, grinning around the cigarette. "You know what he's going to buy first?"

"What?"

"Beef," Spike said. "For the bell peppers and beef. He's going to buy so much beef."

Arthur didn't understand the joke, but he laughed anyway, because Spike was laughing. Something in his chest that had been clenched since the sand let go.

---

**Spike**

He ended up on the stool between the cowboy and the big man with one eye.

The big man looked at him sideways.

"First time?" the big man asked.

"Coming back?" Spike thought about it. "Second, I think. There was a cathedral, once. You?"

The big man held up two fingers.

"Three for me tonight," the priest offered from the end of the bar, "if we're not counting the dozen times I got back up during."

Spike looked at him, then at the big man, then at the cowboy.

"This is a weird bar," he said.

"Yes," said the big man.

"Lovely, though," said the priest.

The bartender set a glass in front of Spike: amber, a little smoky, exactly what he would have ordered if anybody had asked. Spike looked at it and then up at the old man.

"It's paid for," the old man said.

"Yeah," said Spike. "I figured."

He drank. It was good.

They sat for a while, the four of them, in the kind of quiet that only comes after something has ended and before the next thing starts. Arthur coughed now and again. The priest went back to his little book. The big man sat with his hands around his cup like he was warming them at a fire, though there wasn't one.

Then the bartender did something new.

He went down to the far end of the bar, where nobody was sitting, and set down a tall glass. It wasn't whiskey or milk or mead. Whatever was in it was faintly blue and *steaming*, and it smelled sharp and chemical, like hot metal and ozone and a city on fire.

The four of them looked at the glass.

Then they looked at each other.

"Friend of yours?" the priest asked mildly.

Arthur set his whiskey down very slowly.

"Not exactly," he said.

Spike looked at the empty stool in front of the steaming glass for a long moment. Then he grinned.

"Round two, partner?"

Arthur closed his eyes.

"Lord help us," he said.

"Oh, He will," said the priest, rolling up his sleeves.

The big man with one eye reached over without a word and moved his barn-door sword a little closer to his hand.

*From the journal of Arthur Morgan, the page with a sketch of four men at a bar:*

> Strange place. Don't know where it is or how I come to be here, and the old man behind the bar ain't saying. The drinks are good and somebody else is paying, which is two things I can't say about most saloons.
>
> The skinny fella in the blue suit came back. Spike. Says he was born on Mars, whatever that means. Walked right in like he'd stepped out for air. I near cried, which I ain't ashamed to write here since nobody reads this.
>
> There's a priest who heals folks and a big quiet fella with a sword I couldn't lift with both arms. The priest offered to take the sickness off me. I said no. I think he understood. I think the big one did too.
>
> Sent the money where it belongs. Feels good.
>
> The bartender just set out a drink that smells like the inside of a locomotive. I got a feeling I know who it's for.
>
> I don't mind so much. I'd fight him again. Wouldn't be alone this time.
